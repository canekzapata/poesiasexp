Technopoetics of the Anthropocene:
Rendering Our Present through Echoes inMexican Bots and Machines
FRANCISCO GERARDO TIJERINA MARTÍNEZ
WashingtonUniversityinSt.Louis,USA
The Anthropocene is marked with a multilayered environmental crisis that requires
cultural changes. Although a complex task, the contemporary Mexican artists stud-ied here have explored this issue through literary means. In this paper, I analyse La
máquinadistópica (TheDystopianMachine)and Elmonoinfinito (TheInfiniteMonkey)
as technopoetical tools to understand the present from a historical perspective. I suggestthat the usage of canonical figures such as Amparo Dávila and Ada Lovelace in thesepieces provides a critical grounding that tensions the notion of individual authorshipand affords a transhistorical and transtemporal approach to an endangered planet.
Keywords: bots,collaboration,machines,technopoetics,transhistorical,transtemporal.

I aim to read both works as extended authorships and imaginaries of shared collectivities that
seek to portray the ongoing climate emergency, centring fiction as possible means to produce knowl-
edge, while also proposing other ways of relating to the past, the present and the future from a
non-anthropological perspective. I claim that both The Dystopian Machine andThe Infinite Mon-
key, by presenting a re-signifying space for the voices mentioned earlier, allow for a reframing of the
past in order to make the threads of non-linear time visible. In doing so, these projects also provide
afterlives to the works of women like Dávila and Lovelace, proposing a different meaning than the
original, one that speaks to our present and helps us deal with the climate upheaval we are goingthrough. Finally, I suggest that the emphasis of the non-human companions, especially technologicalones, grounds these works in the current era, one marked by rapidly evolving artificial objects andbeings.
As shown by the recent release of the Handbook of Latin American Environmental Aesthetics
(Andermann, Giorgi, and Saramago, 2023), the field of Latin American literary ecocriticism has seen,
with due reason, an increasing amount of attention and cultural production in recent years. Wild-fires, droughts, unbreathable air, rapidly changing weather patterns and the alarming presence of
microplastics in bloodstreams are only some of the concerns that affect Latin American populations.
Additionally, the rising levels of violence towards journalists and activists who seek to stop devel-opment and extractive projects alongside the high levels of impunity for corporations, governmentofficialsandcriminalgroupsinvolvedintheseacts,showhowcrucialitistoaddressthisclimatecrisisfrom different fronts. Thus, I argue that literature has a privileged space, through fictionalisation, to
describe, represent and respond to this multilayered system of violence rooted in anthropocentrism.
In that vein, I read these devices as ascribing pieces to a critical geological era: the Anthropocene.
Crisis in the Anthropocene
The concept of the Anthropocene, coined by Paul Crutzen ( 2006), has been used to designate the
present geological era of the Earth marked by human-made degradation. Although there are many
discussions on the precision and pertinence of this concept, especially because, according to Blanca
de la Torre, its Eurocentric connotations dilute the political, economic and colonial structures embed-ded with the deterioration of the planet (de la Torre in Meseguer’s, 2021: 15), it has undoubtedly
become the most popular way of referring to this age. There has been a wide array of critiquesand emerging concepts that have contested this conceptualisation such as Capitalocene, Chthulucene,
Plantationcene, Eurocene, Technocene, Gynecene and Pyrocene. Although these are imperative in the
production and refinement of knowledge to comprehend the current climate crisis, as Gisela Heffes’pointsout,allthesetermsexplorepracticesthatcontinuethreateningthefluxesofknowledge,biomes,humanandanimalbodiesinmultiplespatialities(Heffes, 2023:130–131).Hence,IuseAnthropocene
here as an umbrella term that encompasses the complexities of the anthropological life, including the
cultural, social, political and economic forces that shape it. Contemporary technologies are deeplyconnected with this concept, as their productions and usage have a direct point of connection to thisissue as it links to a constant resource extraction for its sustainability.
The Anthropocene is an era of crisis. As Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui indicates, this decadent system is
malign and as such there must be a recovery of hidden knowledge linked to ways to address climate,
serviceandsupplyingcrises(RiveraCusicanqui, 2018:92).But,asRiveraCusicanquicontinues,these
crises are also opportunities that can afford a re-examination of inherited discourses and certaintiesthatmakeimpossibletointerrogatewhatweareunwillingtosee(RiveraCusicanqui, 2018:114).Such
interrogation should follow a divergence in the tradition.

Technopoetical Aesthetics
The rise of artificial intelligence has shifted our conversations around pedagogical practices, orig-
inality, crafting and even intelligence itself–this without mentioning the sheer amount of naturalresources,suchaswater,thatsomeofthemostmainstreamservicesuseinordertoanswerourprompts(O’Brien, 2023). Human collaboration with technology is nothing new if we care to take a good look
at history. As Tenen points out, this leads to a conversation around the contrasting duality between
natural and artificial intelligence. How much does our relationship to technology have an impact on
our understanding of intelligence? What is natural and what is artificial? (Tenen, 2024:4 ) .
Tenen questions the mysticism that is often granted to originality and intelligence, as if it were a
purely innate quality of magical conditions that cannot be fully comprehended or understood. For
Tenen, ‘intellect requires artifice, and therefore labor’ (Tenen, 2024: 61–62). The first portion of
Tenen’s statement signals that the duality of the artificial versus human in regard to intelligence hasmore to do with the material conditions of the ‘intelligent’ being and the space these beings occupyin the social scale. The second component allows for a more nuanced approach to said material con-ditions: if intelligence is bound to artifice and therefore to labor, then the resources employed to the
functioning of the machines can be seen as the extracted labor from the planet. Similarly, I claim that
our knowledge is already mediated, shaped and contaminated by our interaction with technology.Tenen’s goal, and mine, is not to discredit human intelligence, but to recognise the sharing practicesthat are crucial for the acquisition and transference of knowledge. The constant evolution of tech-
nology allows for a space of co-threading that not only focuses on individual thriving but also on
collective nurturing.

collaboration in an era of ecological debacle, as I will prove in the following sections of this paper.

the present: ‘Ella, omega, la unidad que fusionará la memoria individual de cada una de ustedes paraconvertirla en Nosotras’ (She, omega, the unity that will fusion the individual memory of each one of
you to make it an Ours) (Riva Palacio Obón, et al., 2021: 32) and ‘Ella, la que baila en una grieta del
espacio-tiempo’ (She, the one who dances on the space–time rift) (Riva Palacio Obón, et al., 2021:
32). Although the English version obscures this quality, the Spanish version has a deliberate use ofgender in its expression of We, granting women a privileged space of collective memory. I wonder
then who is this figure presented in the form of ‘She’. Is it Ada Lovelace or the bot ADA-L? Maybe.
Or it might be whoever recalls her memory or a thread of her thinking, as it is posed in the firstiterationofthetext:‘Tuprimeraabuelafueunaidea,laMedeasimbólicadeAdaLovelace’(Yourfirstgrandmother was an idea, the symbolic Medea of Ada Lovelace) (Riva Palacio Obón, et al., 2021:
26). Furthermore, the text also shows glimpses of two important ideas. On the one hand, the future
fusionofindividualmemoriesintoacollectivememory:aresponsetothehistoricerasureandsystemicviolence that women have suffered through the deployment of the patriarchal, colonial and capitalistapparatuses as systems of domination and extraction. This first idea can be seen as a response to

This commonly built consciousness–or maybe to-be-built or being-built–that Riva Palacio Obón
and ADA-L pursue is the mind of Gaia. In ‘Simbiosis’ (Symbiosis), Riva Palacio and ADA-L resortto the image of the matsutake mushrooms that, according to anthropologist Anna L. Tsing, ‘are wild

But what is the limit of this coexistence? One answer might be in artificial intelligence. In the text

approaches, but the consideration of these technological companions can allow us to extend this line

Conclusion
While the material components of each work studied here differ from one another, both groups ofauthors seem to propose an extended notion of authorship not only by publishing these projects col-lectively, but also by centring other voices at the core of their artworks. Through Amparo Dávila,
Manuel Felguérez and Ada Lovelace, the journey of seeking possible pathways to take during the cli-
mate crisis becomes vital for humans and non-humans alike. This is not to say that we as humankindare the ones who will save the planet, because the planet might keep existing long after humans areextinct.Butthroughmutualcompanionshipandcaretherecanbequalityoflifeforthemultiplespecies
that constitute the ecosystems.
I could not conclude this article without recognising the afterlives that these digital companions
have already had. In the case of ADA-L, Twitter’s purchasing by Elon Musk and the mobilisation ofbots to become a paid feature (Fernholz, 2023) condemned this afterlife of Ada Lovelace to a unidi-
rectionalrelationshipwiththeworld,asshecannotreplytomessagesanylonger(RivaPalacio, 2024).
The symbolic death of ADA-L through silencing can only potentialise her message. Gladly for us, The
InfiniteMonkey continues to extend her life through shared readings and conversations.
